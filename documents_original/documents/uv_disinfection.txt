Ultraviolet (UV) Radiation for Water Disinfection: Principles, Application, and Bacterial Inactivation
 
Ultraviolet (UV) radiation has become one of the most important disinfection technologies in modern water treatment. Unlike chemical disinfectants, UV does not alter the chemical composition of water, does not produce harmful by-products, and is effective against pathogens that are resistant to chlorine, including Cryptosporidium parvum and Giardia lamblia. It is now used in municipal water treatment plants, food and beverage production, pharmaceutical manufacturing, and point-of-use household systems worldwide.

## 1. The UV Spectrum and Germicidal Wavelengths

 Ultraviolet radiation is electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths between 100 and 400 nanometers (nm), outside the visible light range.

| **UV Range** | **Wavelength** | **Germicidal Effect** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UV-A | 315-400 nm | Minimal; causes some indirect DNA damage |
| UV-B | 280-315 nm | Moderate; causes sunburn and some DNA damage |
| UV-C | 200-280 nm | High; primary germicidal range |
| Vacuum UV | 100-200 nm | Extreme; used in advanced oxidation processes |

**Optimal germicidal wavelength: 254 nm** — This wavelength is maximally absorbed by the DNA bases adenine, guanine, cytosine, and thymine (uracil in RNA), causing the greatest degree of photochemical damage per unit of energy.

## 2. Mechanism of Bacterial Inactivation

 UV-C radiation at 254 nm is absorbed directly by bacterial DNA and RNA:

**Primary mechanism — Thymine dimer formation:**
- Adjacent thymine bases on the same DNA strand absorb UV energy and form a covalent bond (cyclobutane pyrimidine dimer or CPD).
- This kink in the DNA strand blocks DNA polymerase from replicating the strand.
- The bacterium cannot divide and is considered inactivated (not necessarily killed, but cannot reproduce).

**Secondary mechanism — Cytosine dimers:**
- Similarly, cytosine-cytosine and cytosine-thymine dimers form, adding to DNA damage.

**Tertiary mechanism — RNA damage:**
- UV also damages messenger RNA (mRNA), preventing protein synthesis.

Key point: UV does not always kill bacteria outright — it renders them unable to reproduce, which is sufficient to prevent infection. Some bacteria can repair UV-induced damage in a process called **photoreactivation** (under visible light) or **dark repair** (without light). This must be considered in UV system design.

## 3. UV Dose Requirements for Key Waterborne Bacteria

 UV Dose = Intensity (mW/cm²) × Time (seconds) = mJ/cm²

**For 4-log (99.99%) inactivation:**

| **Organism** | **UV Dose Required (mJ/cm²)** |
| --- | --- |
| E. coli | 10 |
| Salmonella typhi | 8 |
| Shigella dysenteriae | 6 |
| Vibrio cholerae | 3 |
| Pseudomonas aeruginosa | 55 |
| Klebsiella pneumoniae | 18 |
| Legionella pneumophila | 25 |
| Staphylococcus aureus | 20 |
| Bacillus subtilis spores | 80 |
| Cryptosporidium parvum | 3 |
| Giardia lamblia | 5 |

Key insight: UV is uniquely effective against Cryptosporidium and Giardia, which are highly chlorine-resistant. Pseudomonas requires a much higher dose than most other bacteria.

## 4. UV System Types

### Low-Pressure (LP) UV Lamps
- Emit nearly monochromatic light at 254 nm (germicidal peak).
- Low energy consumption; long lamp life (10,000-16,000 hours).
- Standard for drinking water treatment and small to medium systems.

### Medium-Pressure (MP) UV Lamps
- Emit broad-spectrum UV (200-400 nm).
- Higher intensity; can treat higher flow rates with fewer lamps.
- More effective at photodegradation of organic micropollutants.
- Higher energy consumption; lamp life 4,000-8,000 hours.

### Low-Pressure High-Output (LPHO) Lamps
- 2-3 times the output of standard LP lamps.
- Good balance between energy efficiency and treatment capacity.

### UV-LED Systems
- Emerging technology; no mercury; long lifetime (>20,000 hours).
- Can target specific wavelengths; ideal for point-of-use systems.
- Currently more expensive but costs are decreasing rapidly.

## 5. Factors Affecting UV Effectiveness

| **Factor** | **Effect** |
| --- | --- |
| Turbidity / TSS | Particles block and scatter UV light, shielding bacteria |
| UV transmittance (UVT) | Low UVT water (e.g., colored by iron or organics) absorbs UV before it reaches bacteria |
| Flow rate | Higher flow = shorter exposure time = lower UV dose |
| Lamp fouling | Scale and biofilm on lamp sleeve reduce UV output by 20-50% |
| Temperature | Very low temperatures reduce lamp output |

**Pre-treatment for UV effectiveness:**
- Turbidity must be below 1 NTU for reliable UV performance.
- Iron and manganese removal is critical — both strongly absorb UV.
- Regular lamp sleeve cleaning is essential.

## 6. UV Validation and Monitoring

 UV systems used for drinking water must be validated according to regulatory standards:

- **US EPA UV Guidance Manual:** Requires dose-response testing using challenge organisms.
- **DVGW W294 (Germany) / ÖNORM M 5873 (Austria):** European UV validation standards.
- **Online monitoring:** Continuous measurement of UV intensity (UVI sensor) and water flow rate to calculate delivered dose in real time.
- **Alarm systems:** Flow and UV intensity alarms must immediately notify operators if dose falls below minimum.

## 7. UV in Multi-Barrier Treatment Systems

 UV is most effectively deployed as part of a multi-barrier system:

- **Municipal treatment:** Coagulation → Filtration → UV → Chloramine residual (UV kills Cryptosporidium and viruses; chloramine provides distribution residual).
- **Groundwater disinfection:** UV alone may be sufficient when turbidity is consistently low.
- **Wastewater reuse:** UV + chlorine combined for irrigation or industrial reuse water.
- **Point-of-use systems:** UV after a carbon filter (removes organics that would reduce UVT).

## 8. Advantages and Limitations of UV

| **Advantages** | **Limitations** |
| --- | --- |
| No chemical addition; no taste/odor change | No residual protection after treatment |
| Effective against Cryptosporidium and Giardia | Ineffective against biofilm bacteria |
| No formation of THM or haloacetic acid by-products | Requires low turbidity water |
| Low maintenance; automated systems available | Photoreactivation possible if water exposed to sunlight |
| Effective against chlorine-resistant viruses (adenovirus) | Higher cost than chlorination alone |
