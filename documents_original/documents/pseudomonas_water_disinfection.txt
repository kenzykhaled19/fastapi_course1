 Water Disinfection and Control Methods for Pseudomonas aeruginosa

 Introduction
 Pseudomonas aeruginosa presents unique challenges in water disinfection due to its exceptional environmental adaptability, biofilm-forming capability, and resistance to standard disinfection methods. Unlike most waterborne bacteria, Pseudomonas can grow in low-nutrient water and even in disinfectant solutions. Controlling Pseudomonas in water systems — particularly hospital water networks, cooling towers, and recreational facilities — requires aggressive multi-barrier strategies.

 Why Pseudomonas is Difficult to Eliminate from Water

Biofilm Formation:
 Pseudomonas aeruginosa is one of the most potent biofilm-forming bacteria known. Within hours of contacting a pipe surface, it attaches and begins producing a polysaccharide matrix (alginate) that forms a protective layer. Bacteria within biofilms:
- Receive 100–1000 times less chlorine exposure than free-floating cells
- Are protected from UV radiation
- Can survive chlorine concentrations that would kill planktonic (free-floating) cells in seconds
- Continuously shed cells back into the water flow, causing persistent contamination

 Nutrient Independence:
 Pseudomonas can grow in distilled water, bottled water, and even in antiseptic solutions such as chlorhexidine at low concentrations. It thrives in the oligotrophic (very low nutrient) conditions found in hospital water systems and drinking water distribution networks.

 Disinfection Methods

1. Chlorination
 Free chlorine is moderately effective against planktonic Pseudomonas but largely ineffective against biofilm-associated cells.

 CT values for 99% inactivation of planktonic Pseudomonas at pH 7, 20°C:
- Free chlorine: CT = 0.08 mg·min/L (relatively susceptible when free-floating)
- Against biofilm cells: chlorine concentrations of 100–1000 mg/L may be required — far above safe drinking water levels

 WHO recommended residual chlorine of 0.2–0.5 mg/L in distribution systems is insufficient to eliminate established Pseudomonas biofilms.

Superchlorination (shock chlorination):
 Temporarily raising chlorine to 20–50 mg/L for 1–2 hours can reduce biofilm load, but complete elimination requires physical cleaning first. Used during outbreak situations in hospital water systems.

2. Ultraviolet (UV) Disinfection
 UV at 254 nm is effective against planktonic Pseudomonas at standard doses.

 UV dose for 4-log (99.99%) inactivation of Pseudomonas: 10–15 mJ/cm²
 Standard treatment doses of 40 mJ/cm² far exceed this requirement.

Limitation: UV does not penetrate biofilms and provides no residual protection. UV must be combined with chlorine residual maintenance for distribution system protection.

3. Thermal Disinfection (Hot Flushing)
 Particularly important in hospital hot water systems where Pseudomonas thrives at 25–42°C.
- Hot water storage: maintain above 60°C at all times
- Hot flushing protocol: flush all outlets at 70°C for minimum 30 minutes
- Cold water systems: maintain below 20°C to inhibit growth

Thermal disinfection is one of the most effective methods for eliminating Pseudomonas biofilms in pipe systems.

4. Copper-Silver Ionization
Copper ions (0.2–0.8 mg/L) and silver ions (0.02–0.08 mg/L) are released into water systems. Both ions are highly toxic to Pseudomonas, disrupting enzyme function and cell membrane integrity. Effective against both planktonic and biofilm-associated cells. Widely used in hospital water systems as a supplementary disinfection method.

5. Monochloramine
More stable than free chlorine in distribution systems and shows better penetration into biofilms. Increasingly used as secondary disinfectant in systems with known Pseudomonas biofilm problems.

6. Membrane Filtration
Ultrafiltration (pore size 0.01–0.1 μm) completely removes Pseudomonas at the point of use. Used in hospital immunocompromised patient units (transplant wards, ICUs) where tap water poses infection risk. Filters must be changed regularly as Pseudomonas can colonize filter membranes.

Hospital Water System Management
 Hospitals follow specific Pseudomonas water safety plans:
- Weekly temperature checks at sentinel outlets
- Monthly microbiological testing of water from high-risk areas (ICU, transplant unit, neonatal ward)
- Quarterly biofilm sampling from showerheads and taps
- Immediate replacement of any outlet testing positive for Pseudomonas in immunocompromised patient areas
- Point-of-use filters mandatory in transplant and burns units

Monitoring and Detection
- Culture on cetrimide agar: selective medium for Pseudomonas (produces characteristic blue-green pigment pyocyanin)
- PCR detection: identifies Pseudomonas DNA in water within 4–6 hours
- Recommended action level: any detection of Pseudomonas in water serving immunocompromised patients triggers immediate remediation

Recreational Water Control
 In swimming pools and hydrotherapy pools:
- Maintain free chlorine at 1–3 mg/L (higher than drinking water due to bather load)
- pH 7.2–7.6 for maximum chlorine efficacy
- Shock chlorination weekly with 10 mg/L chlorine for 1 hour
- Regular filter backwashing to remove biofilm accumulation
