Gram-Positive Bacteria in Water: Staphylococcus and Streptococcus — Treatment and Control

While Gram-negative bacteria dominate waterborne disease, Gram-positive bacteria including Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes can also contaminate water supplies and pose significant health risks, particularly in recreational water, hospital water systems, and inadequately treated drinking water. Gram-positive bacteria differ structurally from Gram-negative organisms in having a thick peptidoglycan cell wall and no outer membrane, which gives them different resistance profiles to disinfection.

## 1. Structural Differences Affecting Treatment

Understanding cell wall structure is critical for choosing effective treatment:

| **Feature** | **Gram-Negative** | **Gram-Positive** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peptidoglycan layer | Thin (2-7 nm) | Thick (20-80 nm) |
| Outer membrane | Present (LPS layer) | Absent |
| Chlorine sensitivity | High | Moderate to High |
| UV sensitivity | High | High |
| Desiccation resistance | Low | High |
| Biofilm tendency | High | Moderate |

The absence of an outer membrane in Gram-positive bacteria means they are generally more sensitive to chlorine, as the disinfectant has easier access to the cell interior.

## 2. Staphylococcus aureus in Water

Staphylococcus aureus is a major cause of wound infections, food poisoning, and toxic shock syndrome. In water, it is primarily associated with:

- **Swimming pools and recreational water:** Skin shedding during bathing introduces S. aureus into pool water.
- **Hospital water systems:** Contamination of wound irrigation water or hydrotherapy pools.
- **Bottled water:** Inadequate sanitation of manufacturing equipment.

### Treatment Methods for Staphylococcus

**Chlorination:**
- Free chlorine at 1.0 mg/L with a contact time of 1 minute achieves 4-log inactivation of S. aureus.
- Swimming pool chlorine standard (1.0 to 3.0 mg/L) is effective at controlling S. aureus.
- MRSA (Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus) shows similar chlorine sensitivity as drug-sensitive strains — antibiotic resistance does not confer disinfection resistance.

**UV Radiation:**
- A UV dose of 20 mJ/cm² achieves 4-log inactivation. Highly effective in pool and spa water treatment systems.

**Hydrogen Peroxide:**
- 3-6% hydrogen peroxide solution is used for surface disinfection and equipment sterilization to eliminate S. aureus.

## 3. Streptococcus in Water

Key waterborne streptococcal species:

- **Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Strep):** Causes pharyngitis, scarlet fever, and skin infections. Can be transmitted via contaminated water in swimming pools.
- **Streptococcus faecalis (Enterococcus faecalis):** A traditional fecal indicator organism. Its presence in water confirms fecal contamination.
- **Streptococcus pneumoniae:** Primarily airborne but can survive in water for short periods.

### Treatment Methods for Streptococcus

**Chlorination:**
- Streptococcus species are generally sensitive to chlorine.
- Free chlorine at 0.5 mg/L for 30 minutes achieves greater than 99.99% inactivation.
- Enterococcus (formerly fecal streptococcus) is used alongside E. coli as a co-indicator of fecal contamination in beach water quality monitoring.

**UV Radiation:**
- Highly effective; 10-15 mJ/cm² achieves 4-log inactivation for most Streptococcus species.

**Thermal Treatment:**
- Streptococci are non-spore-forming and are completely destroyed by boiling.
- Pasteurization at 63°C for 30 minutes (or 72°C for 15 seconds) kills all waterborne streptococcal species.

## 4. Advanced Treatment for Recreational and Healthcare Water

| **Application** | **Recommended Treatment** |
| --- | --- |
| Swimming pools | Free chlorine 1-3 mg/L + pH 7.2-7.8 + UV or ozone supplementation |
| Hydrotherapy pools | Higher chlorine dose (3-5 mg/L); more frequent water changes |
| Hospital wound irrigation water | Point-of-use sterile filtration (0.2 micron) |
| Drinking water | Standard coagulation, filtration, and chlorination achieves complete removal |

## 5. Clinical Treatment of Gram-Positive Waterborne Infections

**Staphylococcus aureus:**
- **Sensitive strains:** Flucloxacillin (Nafcillin in USA) is first-line for skin and soft tissue infections.
- **MRSA infections:** Vancomycin IV for serious infections; Linezolid or Daptomycin as alternatives.
- **Toxic shock syndrome:** Vancomycin + Clindamycin (to suppress toxin production) + IV fluids.

**Streptococcus pyogenes (Group A Strep):**
- **First-line:** Penicillin V (oral) or Penicillin G (IV) — Streptococcus pyogenes remains universally sensitive to penicillin.
- **Penicillin allergy:** Erythromycin or Clindamycin.
- **Invasive disease (necrotizing fasciitis):** Emergency surgical debridement + high-dose Penicillin G + Clindamycin.

Note: Unlike Gram-negative waterborne pathogens, Streptococcus pyogenes has not developed clinically significant resistance to penicillin, making it one of the few remaining pathogens reliably treated with this antibiotic.
